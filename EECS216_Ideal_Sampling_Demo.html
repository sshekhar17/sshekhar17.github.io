In this demo we will work through the main steps involved in ideal sampling and reconstruction of bandlimited signals. Unless an interference tone is explicitly added, all plots use the same underlying bandlimited signal.

Increasing Ts decreases ωs, bringing adjacent copies closer. What if they overlap?

2. When replicas overlap: aliasing

Nyquist sampling theorem

The shifted copies can be separated only if the sampling frequency is sufficiently high. When they overlap, the low-frequency portion of the sampled spectrum contains contributions from different frequency bands.

Sampling theorem. If the signal has bandwidth ωB, choosing ωs > 2ωB permits perfect reconstruction by an ideal LPF. When this fails, different continuous-time signals can share the same samples.

Sometimes the sampling rate is adequate for the desired signal, but unwanted high-frequency interference still aliases into its band.

At the sampling instants, cos(2π·60n/50) = cos(2π·10n/50). Once sampled, the interference is indistinguishable from a genuine 10 Hz tone. That is why the filter must be placed before the sampler.

Filtering protects the information before it is lost. Once sampling is fast enough, how do we reconstruct between sample locations?

4. Whittaker–Shannon: reconstruct from sinc functions

Interpolation from discrete samples

At each sample time nTs, place a sinc function centered at that point and scale it by the measured value x(nTs). The sum recovers the original signal when sampling is sufficiently fast and infinitely many terms are included.

Reveal the Whittaker–Shannon formula
x(t) = ∑n∈ℤ x(nTs) sinc((t − nTs)/Ts)

sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu), with sinc(0)=1. Each shifted sinc equals 1 at its own sample time and 0 at all other integer-multiple sample times.

The orange curve uses only the finite sum from n = −K to K; it is approximate even above Nyquist, especially near the plotted boundaries.

Summary: enough samples → separated spectra → ideal low-pass recovery → sinc interpolation in time.
